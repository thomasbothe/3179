Part one / The Gap

Where you are born matters

Life expectancy is not shared equally across the world. While some countries enjoy lifespans well into their 80s, others are far behind.

Decades apart

In Australia, Japan, Canada and much of Western Europe, a newborn can expect to live well into their 80s.

Across much of sub-Saharan Africa, the picture is very different. Life expectancy is often 20 or more years shorter.

Between these extremes lies a broad middle. Latin America, the Middle East and much of Asia fall in the 60s and 70s, closer to wealthier countries than to the lowest-ranked ones, but still substantially lower.

Five years, two realities

Two children are born on the same day. One is born in Nigeria, where about 116 of every 1,000 children will not live to see their fifth birthday. The other is born in Australia, where that number is under 4.

Neither child has done anything to earn their odds.

Across West and Central Africa, early childhood is far more dangerous than almost anywhere else on the planet. In Europe, it is rare for a family to lose a child this young.

Between the extremes, the story is less uniform. Parts of South Asia and the Pacific sit in the middle, and neighbouring countries can look surprisingly different from one another.





Diseases of the old, diseases of the young

Not only does the age at which people die vary, but also how they die.

In countries where the life expectancy exceeds 80 years, people mostly die from non-communicable diseases such as heart disease and cancer.

Contrastingly, in countries with low life expectancies, deaths from infectious diseases such as malaria and HIV/AIDS see a significant spike.

Outcomes vary greatly. But are they improving?

Part two / Change Over Time

Life expectancy is increasing throughout the world

Since 2000, people across the world have gained years of life, though the gains are uneven and a few disruptions have set them back.


Rising, with setbacks

Over the past 25 years, life expectancy has been consistently increasing, with some notable exceptions.

Most countries saw a decline in 2020 and 2021 during covid before continuing to increase again thereafter.

One other notable dip can be seen in 2010 in Haiti as a magnitude 7.0 earthquake hit, killing 300,000.


Big gains, but a long way to go

In most countries, fewer children die before their fifth birthday than in 2015. The biggest falls came where the toll was heaviest.

In Afghanistan, Burundi, Haiti and India, the rate dropped by roughly 15 to 20 deaths for every 1,000 births.

Nigeria is the exception among the high rate countries. Its rate barely moved, from about 118 to 116, and it remains more than double that of any other country on the chart.

At the other end, Australia, Japan, Singapore and much of Europe started low and stayed low, so there was little room left to improve.

Life expectancy is increasing but if we want it to continue to improve, what should we focus on?

Part three / Spending

Spending more is not necessarily the answer

You might expect that countries that focus their resources towards spending on healthcare would experience greater life expectancies. This is not the case though.




Prioritising healthcare spending does not lead to longer lives

The U.S. spends 16.9% of their country's economy on healthcare, leading to people living to 78.4 years on average.

Japan spends 10.8%, and yet people live to 84.1.

Tuvalu, a small Pacific nation, devotes more than a quarter of its economy to healthcare and yet their people still live almost two decades less than the Japanese.




GDP per capita closely follows lifespan

GDP per capita, on the other hand, tends to be a much greater predictor of life expectancy.

As GDP per capita increases, life expectancy also tends to increase, indicating that the resources an individual has access to may be more important to longevity than how much focus their government places on healthcare spending, provided they have access to adequate healthcare resources in the first place.

Money may partially explain life expectancy, but its not the full story. People need access to resources too.

Part four / Resources

Resources Matter

Access to care is more than money. It's whether a hospital has a bed free, and whether a child gets vaccinated. Across the countries shown, both vary enormously, and the gaps don't follow wealth neatly.



Hospital Beds

The number of hospital beds per 1000 people varies dramatically.

Although the countries with the most hospital beds per 1000 people tend to have high life expectancies, a country does not necessarily need a high number of beds to enjoy a high life expectancy.

For example, Australia only has 2.5 hospital beds per 1000 people and has a life expectancy of 83, while Japan has 12.5 beds per 1000 people and has a life expectancy of 84 years.



Vaccination coverage is far from even

For most of the vaccines on this chart, coverage is high where health systems are strong.

In Singapore, Chile, China and Nicaragua, almost every cell is the darkest blue, meaning nearly everyone is vaccinated.

Further down the chart, the colours fade. In Nigeria, Afghanistan, Haiti and Bolivia, even long established vaccines like DPT and polio reach noticeably fewer people.

Covid is the most uneven. Guyana, Moldova and the Solomon Islands sit far below their results for other vaccines, while Nauru does the opposite, with strong Covid coverage despite lower rates elsewhere.





Healthcare follows the people

One thing to note is that national statistics can be deceiving. Although Australia may have great access to resources on average, the story changes when moving into regional and rural areas.

From the map, it's clear to see that major population centres have access to many healthcare resources, while those living in rural QLD, NT, & WA must travel extensive distances to receive healthcare.

Knowing that spending and access to resources can make a difference, where does Australia stand on an intentional level?

Part five / Australia vs World

Australia sits significantly above the global average

Across all key metrics, Australia sits above the global average. So whilst individually these metrics may lead to greater life expectancy, it is likely the combination of them that results in Australians living longer than almost anyone else in the world.



Significantly lower child mortality and greater longevity

The high life expectancy in Australia is the result of two key things.

Firstly, the percentage people born that die before the age of 5 is more than times lower than the global average in Australia.

In addition to fewer young deaths, Australians also tend to live into their 80s and 90s whereas on average around the world people are more likely to die in their late 60s and 70s.





Prosperity across the board leads to longer lives

Australians enjoy significantly above average resources and access to care, all of which culminates in greater health outcomes.

Although the gap is substantial in some categories, life expectancy is only about 10 years higher. This is partly because the world average has been climbing for decades, which has narrowed the distance between the highest life expectancy countries and the rest of the world.

What it all adds up to

A child's chances at life begin at birth.

Born in Australia, you can expect to live to about 83, and almost every child will see their fifth birthday.

Born in Nigeria, you face strikingly different odds.

Money is part of the answer, but not all of it. The United States spends more of its economy on health than any other country wealthy nation, yet its people live years less than many similarly wealthy nations, who spend far less.

What counts is whether care reaches people and resources are available: vaccines are delivered, a hospital is within reach, clean water is available, medical expenses can be paid for.

The countries that do best are the ones that make these resources available for everyone.
