What Keeps Us Well?


Australians born today can expect to live to the age of 83,
among the greatest life expectancy in the world.

However, around the world, outcomes vary drastically.

This visualisation looks at healthcare expenditure, how
people live, and where care can be found.

Part one / Life Expectancy

Who lives longest?

Life expectancy is the average number of years a newborn can expect to live. It is a simple way to compare how healthy whole populations are.

Life expectancy is improving, but not equally

Every country displayed shows an increase in life expetancy between 2015 and 2024.

However, for countries that already had a high life expectancy (> 80), improvements are minimal.

Comparatively, India and Indonesia have substantially increased life expectancy in the same time period.

Where children are most at risk

Each circle is a country, sized by how many children die before their fifth birthday for every 1,000 born (2024).

In Nigeria it is about 116. In Australia it is about 4.

Rates have fallen in many countries since 2015, for example in Afghanistan, from 71.9 to 53.0.

Living longer is the goal. So what do countries put in to get there?

Part two / Resources

Does spending more lead to longer lives?

Health spending is shown as a share of GDP, the total value of everything a country produces in a year. This lets us compare large and small economies fairly.

Australia spends about a tenth of its economy on health

Australia (red) went from 10.1% of GDP in 2015 to 10.8% in 2024. Most countries in this group spend between 10% and 12%, and one stands well apart from the rest.

The biggest spender is not the longest-lived

In 2023 the United States spent 16.9% of GDP and its people lived 78.4 years on average. Japan spent 10.8% and reached 84.1 years. Mexico spent the least (5.7%) and had the lowest life expectancy of the eight.

Follow each country's path

Each line joins one country's years from 2015 to 2024. Where a line climbs, life expectancy rose. Where it moves right, spending rose.

Money matters, but it is not the whole story. What people do, and what is done to protect them, also counts.

Part three / Behaviour and prevention

What do people do, and what protects them?

Two examples: alcohol, a habit shaped by culture, and measles vaccination, a protection delivered by the health system.

Alcohol use differs across countries

Alcohol consumption varies widely across countries, shaped by cultural norms, values, and lifestyles. Australia's average slipped from 10.1 litres per person in 2015 to 9.8 litres in 2023.

Vaccination coverage is far from even

Across the 59 countries on this map, measles vaccination ranges from 54.2% to 99.7%. Australia sits at 92.7%. Darker blue means more people protected.

Habits and prevention happen in communities. To reach them, people need to be able to reach a hospital.

Part four / Access

Where are Australia's hospitals?

Access to hospitals can have a drastic impact on the quality of healthcare people receive. In particular, people living in rural areas have significantly fewer resources available to them.

Hospitals follow the people

Each dot is one hospital. They gather along the south-east coast and around Perth, and thin out sharply inland.

Three states hold three quarters

New South Wales (365), Victoria (257) and Queensland (221) hold about three quarters of the 1,121 hospitals listed. Note these are totals, not hospitals per person.

What the data says

Australians live among the longest lives in this group while spending about a tenth of national income on health. The United States shows that spending more does not guarantee more years, and vaccination and hospital access show that outcomes also depend on how well care reaches people.
